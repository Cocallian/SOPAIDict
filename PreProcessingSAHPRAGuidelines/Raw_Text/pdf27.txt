Guideline for Remuneration of Volunteers in Phase 1 Clinical Trials  
This document has been created to guide applicants/sponsors of clinical trials during the use of registered or unregistered medicines 
in approved clinical trials. It represents the South African Health Products Regulatory Authority’s (SAHPRA) current thinking on 
participant reimbursement during clinical trial participation and proposes a minimum compensation that can be paid. It is not intended 
as an exclusive approach, and SAHPRA reserves the right to request any additional information and may make amendments in 
accordance with the current knowledge at the time.

1. BACKGROUND
According to the National Health Research Ethics Council (NHREC) (2024) and the Council for International 
Organisations of Medical Sciences (CIOMS) (2016) guidelines, clinical trial participants must be 
compensated appropriately for their time (T), inconvenience (I), and expenses (E) [1] [2].  
The South African Health Products Regulatory Authority (SAHPRA) has a clear policy in place for the 
remuneration of participants taking part in phase 2-4 trials [3]. However, there is no such policy in place 
for healthy volunteers participating in phase 1 trials. Most of the Sponsors/Applicants and Pharmaceutical 
Companies/Contract Research Organizations (CRO) are largely following the same guidelines for these 
trials. 
Phase 1 healthy volunteer trials, in which individuals are exposed to the risks of receiving investigational 
products (IPs) with no probability for medical benefit, typically offer more substantial payments than other 
types of clinical research. Additionally, these trials frequently require healthy volunteers to undergo more 
intense medical monitoring, including possible confinement in a residential research facility for days or 
weeks. This raises ethical concerns of coercion with unduly inducing participation with offers of high 
compensation on one hand and exploiting participants with low payments on the other hand [4]. 
The literature is sparse with regard to the compensation of these volunteers, especially in South Africa. 
Elliot and Abadie describe how some healthy individuals use phase 1 trials as their sole source of income 
[5]. While this appears to be primarily a US phenomenon, other countries also report serial phase 1 trial 
participation among healthy volunteers motivated to earn income through clinical trials. An analysis by 
Fisher et al highlighted inequalities and wide variation in phase 1 compensation. The smallest was a 
USD150 vaccine study that did not require any clinical confinement, whereas the largest was a USD13,000 
oncology study that required healthy volunteers to spend 34 consecutive days and nights in the research 
facility [4]. According to WCG, compensation for Phase 1 studies usually works out to be about $10- $20 
an hour.  It is noted that there are some Phase 1 clinical studies, e.g.  studies involving patients with cancer 
or another potentially deadly disease, vaccine studies, and studies involving additions to new age groups 
(such as paediatrics or adolescents), that generally follow the phase 2-4 model because they fall more in 
the clinical care continuum [6]. Phase 1 in this guideline speaks to mainly healthy volunteers exposed with 
no probability for medical benefits. 
With the increase in Phase 1 studies being conducted in South Africa, the need for separate guidelines on 
Phase 1 remuneration is essential to ensure fair volunteer compensation. Three plausible models of 
payment have been discussed – the market model, the wage-payment model, and the reimbursement 
model [7]. However, in the opinion of Grant and Sugarman, the two former models are potentially

2. PURPOSE AND SCOPE
This document guides Applicant/Sponsor/Investigators conducting Phase 1 studies and applies to Phase 1 
studies conducted in South Africa. 
The guideline is only applicable to Phase 1 clinical trials, especially First-In-Human studies. Due to the 
additional risks associated with Gene Transfer Therapy studies, however, this guideline does not apply and 
participant remuneration in these trials will need to be provided on a case-by-case basis. 
3. LEGAL PROVISION
This guideline is established in terms of Regulation 30 of the Medicines and Related Substances Act, 101 
of 1965, on the conduct of clinical trials. 
4. MODEL FOR REIMBURSEMENT OF CLINICAL STUDY-RELATED VISITS
4.1 Time Spent:
Based on the information provided by WCG and factoring in South Africa being a low- to middle-income 
country (LMIC) with a more vulnerable population, R100 per hour is recommended. In general, a 
standard scheduled visit should not take longer than 3 hours. Thus, R300 is recommended for the time 
component of a standard scheduled visit. 
Note: 
4.1.1 
More intensive visits may, however, require additional time at the site. This would necessitate 
a higher “compensation” as the participant is now spending an extended period of time away, 
resulting in them missing the majority of their work/school day as opposed to a portion of it. 
4.2 Inconvenience (Considered in the light of travel/distance from home to a site): 
Based on rates prescribed by the Automobile Association (AA) of South Africa [9], it is recommended 
that R4.75 per km travelled is paid. The travel cost is broken down into 3 categories (0-25, 26-50, and 
>50 km). For example, a participant falling in the first category will receive R237.50 travel compensation
(R4.75 x 25 km x 2 [return trip]). This should be rounded up to R250 for convenience and to make 
provision for any parking costs. 
Note: 
4.2.1 
It would be ideal for each site PI to decide on the radius around the trial site on which

4.3 Expenses: 
It is recommended that all participants be offered a meal to the value of R100. If this amount is included 
in the ICF reimbursement total, then the R100 should be removed from the total, documented in the  
relevant documentation and explained to the participant prior to this adjustment being made. Should 
the participant be at the site for longer than 3 hours, an additional meal or money should be made 
available to them.

4.4 Additional inconvenience: 
Some studies call for more invasive procedures (e.g., PK sampling) or strenuous procedures (e.g., Stress 
ECGs), which are over and above the standard inconveniences of a study visit. Therefore, a case can be 
made for additional compensation. Other inconveniences which are not always so apparent need to 
also be compensated for, such as an HIV trial in which participants are required to abstain from sexual 
activity and product use for specified periods. These additional procedures must be described in the 
Protocol and Informed Consent Form, the additional remuneration specified, and justification of these 
amounts provided. However, if on the visit day the participant reports having not adhered to these 
procedures, the specified amount should be deducted from the overall ICF amount, documented and 
explained to the participant. 
4.5 Scheduled telephonic visits: 
Telephonic visits are generally conducted in one of the following scenarios: 
•
The protocol specifies that a scheduled visit be conducted telephonically as opposed to onsite, or
•
A volunteer is unable to attend the scheduled visit for a specific reason, and the site is instructed
by the protocol and/or CRA to conduct the visit telephonically.
This is still considered to be a trial-related activity, and the participant should thus be remunerated for 
this task. A minimum amount of R250 is recommended. 
4.6 Home-based/virtual consultation visits: 
When permitted by the protocol, a scheduled visit may be conducted either at home or virtually.  The 
participants must be remunerated for time and inconvenience. A minimum of R300 per visit is 
recommended; this could be increased depending on the time and complexity of the visit.  
4.7 Unscheduled visits: 
Unscheduled visits may occur on site, telephonically, virtually, or at home. The same remuneration as 
detailed above applies. 
4.8 Screen Failures: 
A screen failure occurs when a potential volunteer is invited to the site, signs an informed consent, and 
undergoes at least part of a screening visit (such as blood tests, ECG, and/or medical history review), 
but ultimately does not meet the inclusion/exclusion criteria and therefore does not proceed to 
randomization or treatment.  
•
When the screening visit has been concluded in its entirety, the full remuneration is due to the
participant.

When the screen failure is identified before all the screening procedures have been completed
and the patient has not necessarily been allocated a screening number, partial reimbursement for
the visit is expected. This should be 20-50% of the total reimbursement.
4.9 Parent/Legal guardian/caregiver: 
In studies where the participant requires a parent/legal guardian/caregiver to be present, the same 
remuneration scheme applies for both the participant and the parent/legal guardian/caregiver. 
However, the inconvenience of large blood draws, additional sampling or lifestyle restriction 
reimbursement would not be applicable to the parent/legal guardian/caregiver. 
4.10 
Patient diary completion: 
The completion of paper and/or electronic diaries between visits is increasingly being incorporated into 
clinical trials. This amounts to an additional trial-related activity and can be inconvenient and 
burdensome for trial participants to complete these diaries. The participant should thus be 
remunerated for this task. A minimum amount of R100 to R150 per diary entry is recommended, 
depending on the extent of the trial-related procedures (e.g. dosing, temperature measurements, etc) 
that need to be captured.  
In addition, when using electronic diaries, it must be highlighted that most people in South Africa do 
not have access to free wi-fi. Free wi-fi and/or sufficient airtime should also be made available to all 
participants to complete the necessary trial-related activities. Alternatively, sponsors can provide 
devices that have data preloaded. 
4.11 
Electronic Clinical Outcome Assessments (eCOA), including the use of tablets, 
smartphones, wearables, and other such devices: 
As for 4.10 above, participants need to be remunerated appropriately for this task. In addition, 
sufficient airtime needs to be made available to all participants, and/or a device with pre-loaded data 
need to be provided by the Sponsor. 
4.12 
In-patients: 
Volunteers are frequently required to be confined in a residential research facility as part of their 
clinical trial participation for a few hours or even days. This can be burdensome and inconvenient to 
the volunteer and may involve missing work/school and/or incurring additional costs such as 
babysitting or child-minding.  
4.12.1 For in-patient stays up to 12 hours, an amount of R150 per hour is recommended. In addition, 
all volunteers must be provided with standard meals, relevant transport costs, and
