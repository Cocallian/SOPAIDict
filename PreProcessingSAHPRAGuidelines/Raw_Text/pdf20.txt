GUIDELINES TO CLINICAL TRIALS IN PREGNANCY AND 
LACTATION 
This document has been prepared to serve as a guideline for the preparation of applications to conduct clinical trials in South Africa. 
It represents the South African Health Products Regulatory Authority’s [SAHPRA] current thinking on pregnancy and lactation for 
clinical research in South Africa. It is not intended as an exclusive approach, and SAHPRA reserves the right to request any additional 
information and may make amendments in keeping with the knowledge that is current at the time.

1. 
INTRODUCTION 
All clinical trials related to pregnancy and lactation need to be rigorously scientifically, ethically, statistically, 
and clinically sound and appropriate to both the needs of this group of participants and important to the needs 
of the South African and the rest of the world thereby ensuring maximal safety and minimal risk as well as 
critical benefits to Women of childbearing age, foetuses and neonates/ infants.  
These studies therefore need to be in conformance with the latest and current guidelines regarding Good 
Clinical Practice SAGCP, National Department of Health Guidelines for Ethics and Research as well as the latest 
Declaration of Helsinki, ICH-GCP and CIOMS as well as other relevant clinical research guidelines. 
Clinical research with individuals, groups, or communities in situations of vulnerability is only justified if it is 
responsive to both their health needs and priorities as well as those of groups and communities who stand to 
benefit from the resulting knowledge, practices, or interventions. 
Such studies further need to conform to the UN Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the Constitution of the 
Republic of South Africa, and relevant laws and regulations in the Republic.  
While pregnancy is not considered a disease or illness, it needs to be recognized that there are physiological 
and psychological changes in pregnancy, foetal and neonatal/ infant milieus, which may exhibit unique PK and 
PD parameters. There may be unique pathological conditions contributing to the need to study interventions 
in these participants.  
1.1 
Purpose 
There is a need to be more inclusive, and retention of, with pregnancy and lactation related interventional 
trials involving investigational products (new and existing chemical entities) and medical devices and IVDs 
whilst not compromising safety, participant benefit, and protection of the participant, in all studies, Phases 
1 to 4. Studies in this domain can be planned either as independent study or a sub study or as continued 
pregnancy follow up embedded within studies or sub studies with other objectives.  
1.2 
Scope    
This guideline applies to all clinical trials (Phase 1-4). It establishes a framework for:  
• 
Protection of participants in clinical trials related to pregnancy and lactation (maternal, foetal and 
neonatal/ infant).  
• 
Enabling appropriate conduct and successful outcome of clinical trials in pregnancy and lactation.

• 
To guide stakeholders on clinical trials during pregnancy and lactation.  
• 
To guide management of participants' outcomes for participants who become pregnant and/or lactate 
during clinical trials not primarily designed for reproductive health. 
In case of studies involving medical devices/ IVDs (e.g., diagnostic assays and kits, monitoring systems), refer 
also to the Guidelines on clinical investigation requirements on https://www.sahpra.org.za/medical-devices-
and-in-vitro-diagnostics-guidelines. 
2. 
LEGAL PROVISION 
These guidelines are established in terms of Regulations issued in sections 90(s) and 72(6)(c) of the National 
Health Act, Act No. 61 of 2003, as well as Regulation 30 of the Medicines and Related Substances Act, Act 
No. 101 of 1965. 
All role players involved in clinical trials related to pregnancy and lactation should also be familiar with the 
Constitution of the Republic of South Africa, national and international guidelines, including but not limited 
to the following current versions or their successors: 
• 
SA GCP

3. 
PREGNANCY 
3.1 
Pre-submission Meetings 
Stakeholders should, at both regulatory request and/ or self-decision, engage with SAHPRA to ensure 
aspects of the planned study are discussed for smooth progress and regulatory success (refer to 
communications with SAHPRA guidelines).

Pregnant or breastfeeding women should be included in human clinical trials where possible, i.e., when 
safety and other relevant information are available during a clinical trial. This is to ensure that appropriate 
knowledge about NCEs for this group of people is developed. 
3.2 
Public Health Emergencies 
The recent COVID and HIV pandemics have illustrated the need for adequate study and planning of the use 
of IPs in pregnancy and lactation.  
Studies that address the scenarios of public health emergencies will be prioritized at the CTC level to 
expedite early implementation. 
More frequent vigilance in terms of early SAE/ AESI reporting or progress reports or applicant engagement, 
or interim analyses by DSMBs or sponsors may be required at regulatory discretion.  
3.3 
Prevailing Evidence (Non-clinical/ Clinical) 
In preparation for human studies, non-clinical developmental and reproductive safety and toxicology 
studies must be done along with genotoxic studies. These studies must be available for any SAHPRA 
submission for evaluation. 
All clinical studies in pregnant women require clinical data in non-pregnant participants before extending 
to pregnant participants. Outcomes of these studies will guide extension to pregnancy and lactation.  
As there are maternal and fetal differences in each trimester, any study needs to take cognisance of the 
appropriateness of the study design per trimester.  
3.4 
Clinical studies incorporating pregnancies 
In specific, predetermined, pre-discussed situations, clinical studies may progress directly to pregnant and 
lactating participants. 
If the applicant/ sponsor determines that proceeding with trials in pregnancy is appropriate, then the 
following approaches/ actions (in no specific order) need to be considered and/ or incorporated into the 
development strategy: 
• 
Recruitment of pregnant individuals in each trimester into ongoing and/ or subsequent clinical 
trials, 
• 
Removal of mandatory contraception requirements in ongoing and/or subsequent clinical trials,

• 
Implementation of study(ies) specifically designed to be conducted in pregnant individuals if 
needed, 
• 
Ongoing participation of individuals who become pregnant during clinical trials (source: ICH E21 
Guidelines), and 
• 
Management of pregnancies where contraceptive methods have failed requires a safety review 
before concluding on the risk-benefit of the IP. 
Pregnant individuals must continue in the study but not receive any further IP (unless otherwise specified 
in the protocol or if there is a demonstrated positive risk-benefit). 
The baby should be followed up for an appropriate period, preferably up to a minimum of 18 months. 
3.4.1 
Scenario of Failed Contraception in Studies where WOCBP are enrolled with 
mandatory contraception  
Women of childbearing potential may experience a “breakthrough pregnancy” despite the implementation 
of a trial-required method of contraception. Several outcomes may be presented depending on an informed 
choice and intensive information sharing by the investigator and/ or sponsor, such as: 
• 
Continuation of pregnancy with or without IP under strict(er) investigator/ sponsor guidance and 
surveillance until birth and close evaluation of infants thereafter, 
• 
Termination of Pregnancy and no continuation in the study, 
• 
Possible unblinding, 
• 
Reporting to the regulatory authority, 
• 
Re-evaluation of participant insurance if needed, and 
• 
Pregnant participants should be retained in studies as far as possible, and allowance should be 
made to incorporate obstetric and post-natal and paediatric care within the study. 
3.5 
Phase 1 Studies - (Refer to first-in-human, Phase I guideline) 
3.5.1 In exploratory Phase 1 studies, (SAD and MAD) women should typically be of “non-childbearing 
potential”. (ABPI). 
3.5.2 Generally, Phase I trials should not be done in pregnant women.

3.5.3 
Should the trials be required in pregnant women, they must be performed at a later stage of 
clinical IP(s) development once there is data from non-pregnant participants. 
 
3.6 
Procedures 
Procedures should be carried out at minimal risk and at the very least within the standards of antenatal 
care. The following items should be summarized and further described and included in the site capacity as 
per Section 2.4 of the new trial application (CTF1). The SOP should further be provided for the items below: 
• 
An antenatal and postnatal care plan must be provided,  
• 
 Heath care professionals experienced in ultrasound should be available, 
• 
Midwives and obstetricians as well as other Health care professionals with adequate experience 
should be available for delivery, 
• 
Site should include investigators with experience in neonatal management, 
• 
Participants should have easy access to antenatal and labour care, 
• 
Procedural obstetrics (Chorionic villous sampling, amniocentesis) should be done if clinically 
indicated, 
• 
Cord blood to be taken only after delayed cord clamping, 
• 
Adhere to standardized blood volume draws as per the Ethics committees or SAHPRA CTC 
review, 
• 
Infant follow-up should be with qualified personnel and 
• 
Appropriate emergency trolley as per SAHPRA guidelines must be available on site 
3.7 
Sites and Investigators  
3.7.1 Clinical trial sites should have investigators who are obstetricians/ diplomates or proficient in the 
management of obstetric emergencies. Sites should be set up to manage these or have quick and 
established access to a facility with expertise and capacity to manage such outcomes. 
3.7.2 Sonography has become an integral part of the standard of antenatal care- hence demonstrated 
experience and/or qualification needs to be provided in the CTF1 Section 2.4.

3.7.3 The inclusion of neonatologists and/or paediatricians should be considered where appropriate in the 
continuum of care. 
3.7.4 Any study that involves pregnant women needs to have a formal SOP on where and how deliveries 
will be managed. The applicant should propose and justify, based on the complexities of the study, 
whether incorporation of an obstetrician and /or paediatrician and neonatologist may be necessary.  
3.8 
Registration of Investigational Product 
SAHPRA CTC will only consider pregnancy/lactation-related studies where there is a potential benefit to the 
South African population. 
3.9 
Foetal/ Neonatal and Infant extension 
3.9.1 Many studies in pregnancy and lactation may have objectives and endpoints (or estimands) related 
to the foetus and neonate or infant. 
3.9.2 In studies where there are extensions to monitoring the outcomes of neonates and infants, as 
incorporated in the study design, these studies should include investigators with experience in 
paediatrics and/ neonatology and incorporate (both parent/ caregiver) TIE and insurance for the 
neonate/ infant, as well as clinical care for the duration of the study.   
3.10 Pregnancy studies with benefit to the Neonate/ Infant 
3.10.1 Pregnancy (or maternal) studies may be justified to have value for maternal outcomes, or maternal-
foetal, or maternal-infant outcomes. The Ethics thereof are evolving but advanced, especially as 
regards vaccine studies in the maternal population. SAHPRA recognizes that IPs are also being 
evaluated in this population. 
3.10.2 SAHPRA CTC encourages the implementation of such studies on CTC review. 
3.11 Retention of obstetric and paediatric records 
In South Africa, child health records should be retained at least until the child's 21st birthday, as legally, 
minors have until three years after reaching the age of majority to make claims. This applies to various 
health records, including those related to obstetric care. Additionally, records should generally be kept for 
at least six years after they become dormant (date of last contact). Records for patients with mental 
incapacities should be kept for their lifetime.

4. 
LACTATION 
4.1 
Breastfeeding Support 
4.1.1 It is recognised that breastfeeding support and successful initiation and prolongation of 
breastfeeding in South Africa require a sustained collaborative effort to improve, as evidenced by the 
Tshwane declaration in support of breastfeeding (2011). 
4.1.2 In the context of clinical studies, investigational products can potentially be present in breastmilk and 
affect the neonate or infant. It is thus necessary to include studies or initiate studies on drug 
metabolism and excretion during lactation for infant safety.   
4.1.3 Although it is accepted that “breast is best”, outcomes of lactation research may indicate that, in 
certain circumstances, alternatives to breastfeeding might be an option to avoid exposure to drugs 
in breastmilk. 
4.1.4 Infrastructural support is strongly recommended by providing safe, private spaces at clinical trial 
centers with lactation consultant support if needed. Cultural/ Religious and Traditional practices and 
beliefs must be considered in the design of lactation studies. 
4.1.5 If required, the inclusion of paediatricians and lactation consultants may be necessary.   
4.1.6 Lactation studies in South Africa and other LMICs should be supported 
4.2 
Objectives  
Ultimately, clinical guidance on drug concentrations in breastmilk is driven by 3 outcomes: 
• 
The concentration of the IP in breastmilk 
• 
The dose of exposure to the infant  
• 
The safety (and possibly benefit) to the infant  
Breastmilk is a dynamic human tissue. Characteristics differ from colostrum to foremilk, hindmilk, 
premature, and term milk, with intraday variations. Thus, it is recommended that protocol norms be 
established for uniformity in the collection of specimens.

4.3 
Inclusion of lactating mothers in studies 
• 
Non-nursing mothers who are still producing breastmilk (but not breastfeeding their infant) may 
be included in lactation studies. 
• 
Mothers who are breastfeeding: To assess drug exposure in breastmilk and breastfed infants. 
• 
Extension of pregnancy studies to the lactation period. 
• 
Temporary cessation of breastfeeding and replacement with formula will not be allowed. 
4.4 
Safety and Efficacy monitoring  
4.4.1 The same principles apply with respect to the safety monitoring of lactating mothers and their 
infants. The table of visit schedules should adequately consider this in the protocol. 
4.4.2 In addition, certain conditions unique to the neonatal period may require extra consideration 
(growth, development, neonatal pathology). 
4.4.3 Neurodevelopmental outcomes may be of consideration for long-term follow-up. 
4.4.4 For studies where there is maternal intervention for infant benefit, such as vaccine studies, more 
information may be obtained by analysis of breastmilk.  
4.4.5 Causality with SAEs/ AEs/ AESIs must be interpreted within the context of plausible exposure to IP. 
4.4.6 Termination of studies in lactation will be guided by Safety Review committees, DSMBs, and 
regulatory authorities. 
4.5 
Breastmilk Volumes 
4.5.1 Standardized reference values for volumes of breastmilk collection in clinical studies are not 
available. 
4.5.2 The minimum amount of breastmilk for the indicated study should be utilized for optimal PK/ PD 
analysis, or any other analysis. 
4.5.3 Breastmilk is considered Human Tissue in South Africa (National Health Act 61 of 2003), and all 
biological hazard precautions in acquisition, processing, storage, and transport should be taken.  An 
export licence and material transfer agreement will be necessary for the export of the specimen.
