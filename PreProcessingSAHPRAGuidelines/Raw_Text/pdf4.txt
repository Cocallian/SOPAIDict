GUIDELINE FOR BORDERLINE PRODUCTS  
This guideline provides information on the process to be followed when the appropriate regulatory pathway 
for a specific product is unclear. It also outlines the criteria to be applied to determine whether the product 
is regulated in terms of the Medicines and Related Substances Act, 1965 (Act 101 of 1965). It should be 
noted that regulatory decisions relating to borderline products will be made on a case-by-case basis. 
Applicants are urged to comply with the administrative requirements when submitting applications relating 
to borderline products to facilitate efficient processing. The South African Health Products Regulatory 
Authority reserves the right to request additional information where necessary.

1. 
INTRODUCTION 
Health products that aim to diagnose, treat, or prevent disease and injury include all medicines and medical 
devices (including in vitro diagnostic devices). Boundaries may sometimes be blurred, with products coming to 
market which are difficult to distinguish as a medicine, medical device, cosmetic, disinfectant, or foodstuff. A 
lack of clarity in such cases may lead to overlap or conflicting regulatory requirements. In some cases, the 
product may escape regulation with no legislation specific to that product being applied. 
According to the World Health Organization (WHO), “borderline products are generally medical products for 
which it is unclear as to which legislation applies. Although such products may have some of the attributes of 
two or more categories of regulated products, they are not necessarily combination products.” The WHO 
advises that “to be predictable and transparent, the regulator should develop criteria and mechanisms for 
determining the appropriate regulatory regime for such products through a guideline. It should describe 
considerations and the process whereby an applicant may obtain an advisory opinion from the regulatory 
authority.”1 Where necessary, the process should allow for consultation with subject matter experts, the 
manufacturer concerned, as well as with other regulators as needed. It may also consider determinations made 
by regulatory authorities in other jurisdictions.  
1.1 Purpose 
This guideline sets out the process to determine the appropriate regulatory pathway to be applied in the 
regulation of borderline products. This approach will help to provide applicants with routing to the relevant 
competent authority and avoid “regulatory orphaning” for products not clearly regulated under the 
Medicines and Related Substances Act, 1965 (Act 101 of 1965) (the Medicines Act), or the Foodstuffs, 
Disinfectants and Cosmetics Act, 1972 (Act 54 of 1972) (the Foodstuffs, Cosmetics & Disinfectants Act). 
1.2 Scope 
This document provides guidance on borderline products which could either be classified as medicines or 
medical devices under the Medicines Act, products classified as cosmetics, disinfectants or foodstuff under 
the Foodstuffs, Cosmetics and Disinfectants Act as well as other consumer products which are not regulated 
by these two pieces of legislation. The scope includes combination products which, for purposes of this 
guideline, may contain a scheduled substance, medicine, and/or a medical device. This guideline does not 
include products that are, for example, regulated under the Fertilizers, Farm Feeds, Agricultural Remedies

2. 
LEGAL PROVISIONS 
According to Section 2A of the Medicines Act, the objects of SAHPRA are to monitor, evaluate, regulate, 
investigate, inspect, register, and control medicines, scheduled substances, clinical trials, medical devices, 
including in-vitro diagnostics (IVDs), and related matters in the public interest. The legal definitions of a 
“medicine, medical device, and a scheduled substance” are provided in Section 1 of the same Act, while 
Section 15 provides for the registration of medicines, medical devices, and IVDs. 
If SAHPRA is satisfied that a medicine or medical device should be registered in terms of section 15(3)(a) of 
the Medicines Act, SAHPRA may register the medicine or medical device subject to conditions determined 
in terms of section 15(6). Before embarking on the process of application for registration of a product, a 
decision may, however, be needed as to whether the product in question does indeed fall within the ambit 
of the Medicines Act. This guideline has been developed to guide that decision-making process. 
It must be noted, however, that products regulated under both the Medicines Act and the Foodstuffs, Cosmetics 
and Disinfectants Act may in addition to specific regulatory control under these Acts, also be regulated by aspects 
of other legislation including but not limited to: 
• 
the National Health Act, 2003 (Act 61 of 2003); 
• 
the Hazardous Substances Act, 1973 (Act 15 of 1973); 
• 
the National Regulator for Compulsory Specification Act, 2008 (Act 4 of 2008); or 
• 
the Legal Metrology Act, 2014 (Act 9 of 2014). 
In addition, all products which fall under the definition of ‘goods’ as defined in the Consumer Protection Act 
2008 (Act 68 of 2008) are also regulated by that Act. 
3. 
HEALTH PRODUCTS 
3.1 
Medicine 
A medicine is defined in the Medicine Act as: 
“(a) 
any substance or mixture of substances used or purporting to be suitable for use or 
manufactured or sold for use in—

(i) 
the diagnosis, treatment, mitigation, modification, or prevention of disease, abnormal 
physical or mental state or the symptoms thereof in humans; or 
(ii) restoring, correcting, or modifying any somatic or psychic, or organic function in humans, 
and 
(b) 
includes any veterinary medicine.” 
A substance or mixture of substances may be classified as a medicine based on what it is used for, or is 
claimed to be suitable for use for, as per the definition provided above. The action of a medicine may be 
achieved in, or on the body of a human or animal, by one or more of the following, or any other mechanism: 
• 
Pharmacological - meaning an interaction typically at a molecular level between a substance 
or its metabolites and a constituent of the human body which results in the initiation, 
enhancement, reduction, or blockade of physiological functions or pathological processes; 
• 
Immunological - meaning an action in or on the body that occurs through the stimulation 
and/or mobilisation of cells and/or products involved in a specific immune reaction; or 
• 
Metabolic - meaning an action which involves an alteration of the normal chemical processes 
participating in, and available for, normal body function. Such an action could involve the 
substance or one or more of its metabolites stopping, starting or changing the rate, extent or 
nature of a biochemical process, whether physiological or pathological.  
The fact that a product is itself metabolised does not, however, imply that it achieves its principal intended 
action by metabolic means. 
3.2 
Medical devices 
A medical device is defined in the Medicines Act as: 
“Any instrument, apparatus, implement, machine, appliance, implant, reagent for in vitro use, 
software, material, or other similar or related article, including Group III and IV Hazardous Substances 
contemplated in the Hazardous Substances Act, 1973 (Act 15 of 1973)— 
(a) intended by the manufacturer to be used, alone or in combination, for humans or animals, for 
one or more of the following:  
(i) 
diagnosis, prevention, monitoring, treatment, or alleviation of disease;

(ii) 
diagnosis, monitoring, treatment, alleviation of, or compensation for an injury; 
(iii) 
investigation, replacement, modification, or support of the anatomy or of a physiological 
process; 
(iv) 
supporting or sustaining life; 
(v) 
control of conception; 
(vi) 
disinfection of medical devices; or 
(vii) 
providing information for medical or diagnostic purposes by means of in vitro 
examination of specimens derived from the human body; and 
(b) which does not achieve its primary intended action by pharmacological, immunological or 
metabolic means, in or on the human or animal body, but which may be assisted in its intended 
function by such.  
Where a health product does not achieve its primary intended purpose by pharmacological, immunological, 
or metabolic means, the product is designated as a medical device, e.g. coronary stent. Such a product may, 
however, be assisted in that effect or purpose by pharmacological, immunological, or metabolic means, 
e.g., a coronary stent coated with a scheduled substance, referred to as a drug eluting stent (DES). 
A product that is a “related article,” is included in the definition of medical device e.g. operating software 
in a medical device or a foot switch which is necessary to operate a medical device, is itself classified as a 
medical device.  
3.3 
Combination products 
In the case of medicines and medical devices, a combination product means a product which may be 
comprised of two or more components regulated as a medicine and a medical device e.g. a syringe 
prefilled with a medicine. These components may be physically, chemically, or otherwise combined or 
mixed and presented as a single entity. A medical device may contain a medicine or a scheduled substance 
which acts on the body in a manner ancillary to the device. However, where such a substance(s) acts in a 
manner that is the primary intended mode of action, the product is regulated as a medicine rather than a 
medical device e.g. medicine provided in a metered dose inhaler to treat asthma. Refer to SAHPGL 
Guideline for Classification of Medical Devices.

4. 
NON-HEALTH PRODUCTS 
4.1 
Cosmetic 
A cosmetic is defined in the Foodstuffs, Cosmetics and Disinfectants Act, 1972 as: 
“Any article, preparation or substance (except a medicine as defined in the Medicines and Related 
Substances Act, 1965 (Act 101 of 1965)) intended to be rubbed, poured, sprinkled or sprayed on or 
otherwise applied to the human body, including the epidermis, hair, teeth, mucous membranes of the 
oral cavity, lips and external genital organs, for purposes of cleansing, perfuming, correcting body 
odours, conditioning, beautifying, protecting, promoting attractiveness or improving or altering the 
appearance, and includes any part or ingredient of any such article or substance;” 
Cosmetics are applied to external parts of the human body with the intended purpose as included in the 
definition. There is a broad range of products at the cosmetic-medicine-medical device interface. A 
therapeutic claim may not be made for a cosmetic. In addition, consideration must be given to whether the 
product is being used, purporting to be suitable for use or manufactured or sold for use for the purposes 
described in the definition of a medicine or medical device. If such a claim is made and depending on the 
representation of its intended use and/or composition, the provisions of the Medicines Act may apply.  
In cases where there is doubt, the appraisal should focus on whether the product is a medicine or a medical 
device. Thus, if the product falls within the definition of a medicine or a medical device and/or a therapeutic 
claim is made (e.g. reference made ‘to treat or prevent skin disease’), it must be assessed under the 
medicine or medical device framework. If neither a medicine nor a medical device framework is applicable 
it could, for example, be classified as a cosmetic.  
4.2 
Disinfectant  
A disinfectant is defined in the Foodstuffs, Cosmetics and Disinfectants Act, 1972 as: 
“Any article or substance used or applied or intended to be used or applied as a germicide, 
preservative or antiseptic, or as a deodorant or cleansing material which is not a cosmetic.” 
A sanitising product may be classified as a medicine, medical device or disinfectant depending on the: 
(a) intended use and function of the product; 
(b) composition of the product; 
(c) surface to which it is applied e.g. the skin of a person or animal or an inanimate surface; and 
SAHPGL-CRO-01_v1

(d) the environment where the product is intended for use. 
For example, a hand sanitiser for personal hygiene might be considered to be a cosmetic if marketed only for 
cleansing, but if claims are made that it is antibacterial, it may be classified as a disinfectant or medicine. If, 
the product’s claims or use align with any human or veterinary therapeutic intent, it is a medicine rather than 
a disinfectant. Disinfectants used to clean medical instruments and inanimate surfaces in for example, 
hospitals, operating rooms, intensive care units and burn units will be controlled as medical devices under 
the Medicines Act.  
4.3 
Foodstuff  
A foodstuff is defined in the Foodstuffs, Cosmetics and Disinfectants Act, 1972 as: 
“Any article or substance (except a medicine as defined in the Medicines and Related Substances Act, 
1965 (Act 101 of 1965)) ordinarily eaten or drunk by a person or purporting to be suitable, or 
manufactured or sold, for human consumption, and includes any part or ingredient of any such article 
or substance, or any substance used or intended or destined to be used as a part or ingredient of any 
such article or substance; 
In cases where there is doubt, the first consideration must be whether or not the product falls within the 
definition of a medicine. Products marketed as foods or supplements should not make therapeutic claims 
as doing so will result in classification as a medicine. 
4.4 
Other consumer products 
In addition, there are other products which do not meet the definition of either a medicine, medical device, 
foodstuff, cosmetic, or disinfectant and are intended for purposes or uses not covered by these product 
types. Such products may include, but are not limited to, exercise equipment, software applications and 
programmes, and information and communication technology. If, however, a product such as a fitness 
tracker has the functionality of a medical device (e.g. electrocardiogram (ECG) monitoring in watches) it 
should be regulated as a medical device.

5. 
BORDERLINE PRODUCTS 
The process for classification of a borderline product should be initiated when there is a lack of clarity or 
dispute regarding the correct regulatory pathway to be followed for the classification of the product. In dealing 
with these situations, the first step is to decide whether or not the product is regulated under the Medicines 
Act. If it is determined that the product should be regulated by SAHPRA, a recommendation may be made 
regarding the appropriate regulatory pathway to be applied. 
5.1 
Criteria 
In order to decide the regulatory pathway appropriate for a borderline product, the following factors will be 
considered: 
(a) The legal definitions of a medicine, medical device, foodstuff, cosmetic, and disinfectant as 
provided in the Medicines Act, and the Foodstuffs, Cosmetics and Disinfectants Act, MUST be 
considered first. In addition, it must be ascertained whether the product itself or a component 
of the product has been called up for registration in terms of section 14(2) of the Medicines Act. 
It is also important to check whether the product or a component thereof has been declared as 
undesirable in terms of section 23 of the Medicines Act in the case of medicines for human use, 
or section 36A in the case of veterinary medicines. 
(b) Consideration will also be given to the primary intended purpose and/or use of the product, 
including any claim(s) made for the product. Both explicit and implicit claims are considered 
when a product is classified. Explicit claims are stated clearly with no ambiguity regarding their 
meaning or intent. In contrast, implicit claims use wording which may suggest a therapeutic 
benefit in an indirect manner (for example -Product X is ‘clinically proven’).  
Even if no therapeutic claim is made (either explicit or implicit), a product may be classified as a 
medicine or a medical device, if the intrinsic properties of the product are such that there is no 
other possible use. For example, paracetamol has no other use but as a medicine. 
When considering the claim made for a product, it is important to note whether more than one 
property can contribute to the overall effect of the product when used for this indication. To 
make the necessary distinction, the applicant should, where appropriate do a risk assessment. 
Specifically, if it is determined that the greater risk is associated with the modification of any 
somatic, psychic, or organic function, then the safety, quality, and efficacy of the product would 
be more appropriately assessed under the medicine framework. The reverse will also apply.

(c) The method by which the primary mode of action is achieved, e.g., through pharmacological, 
immunological, or metabolic means must be considered. The primary mode of action of the 
product means the most important therapeutic action that makes the greatest contribution to 
the overall intended therapeutic effect(s) of the product. For example, the primary mode of 
action of a metered dose inhaler containing a scheduled substance used to treat asthma, is the 
pharmacological action of the scheduled substance included in the product, and thus it is 
regulated as a medicine. In another example, however, the primary mode of action of a drug-
eluting stent, is for the stent to keep the blood vessel open. The scheduled substance coated 
onto the stent has the secondary role of reducing the risk of blood clotting in the blood vessel. 
The product is thus regulated as a medical device. 
(d) The composition of the product - the presence of, for example, a scheduled substance, in a 
product that is making a therapeutic claim, would make the product unsuitable for designation 
as a cosmetic or foodstuff.  
(e) The classification of similar products on the market and/or in other regulatory jurisdictions may 
be considered by SAHPRA but will not be binding. It must be emphasised that classification is 
done on a case-by-case basis with that applied in other jurisdictions used for guidance only, with 
final decisions based on South African legislation and context. 
The criteria above may not be given equal weight in support of a decision, as some factors may 
be more important to consider in one circumstance versus another. Not all criteria need to be 
applied if a decision can be reached after the examination of one or a few criteria. If the standard 
criteria do not lead to a clear decision, supplementary considerations may be considered. The 
final decision is dependent on the overall consideration. See decision tree in Annexure A 
Once a product has been classified as a medicine or medical device in terms of the Medicines Act, further 
classification would be applied in accordance with the Act, regulations, and applicable guidelines as 
determined by SAHPRA. For example, a medicine could be classified into a category as provided in 
Regulation 9 of the General Regulations published in terms of the Medicines Act. A medical device would 
be further classified in accordance with SAHPGL-MD-04 Classification of Medical Devices and IVDs. 
5.2 
Request for classification of a borderline product 
The process for classification of a borderline product may be initiated by a prospective applicant before the 
submission of an application for registration of a health product in terms of the Medicines Act. Alternatively, 
the process may be proposed to the applicant by SAHPRA after consideration of an application for

registration of a product. In either case, the applicable fee is payable by the applicant. 
Recommendations as to whether a borderline product falls under the jurisdiction of the Medicines Act are 
made by a Technical Advisory Committee (TAC) appointed by the Chief Executive Officer of SAHPRA. The 
TAC will consist of representatives of relevant units within SAHPRA and external experts in the areas of 
foodstuffs, cosmetics, and disinfectants. 
SAHPRA will provide the decision to the applicant as to whether the product is deemed to be a medicine, 
medical device or scheduled substance regulated under the Medicines Act. If it is deemed to fall outside 
the jurisdiction of SAHPRA, a recommendation may be made to the applicant regarding on an appropriate 
alternative entity e.g. the Food Control Directorate of the National Department of Health for further input. 
SAHPRA may change its view regarding the classification of the product in the event of any information or 
evidence that has a bearing on the status of the product. This may relate to a change in the legislation 
relating to the product (for example definitions), a change in scientific knowledge or even the way in which 
the product is packaged, promoted, or presented. SAHPRA can provide no assurance that any product, 
including products under development, will not subsequently be reclassified (for example, as a medicine or 
medical device). SAHPRA will engage with the holder of the certificate of registration, or the applicable 
licence holder regarding such a change in classification. 
SAHPRA must also be informed if there is an intention to make a change to the claims made relating to a 
product or to the composition thereof.

5.3 
Regulatory compliance 
In cases where a product is being marketed which may be considered to be a health product but is not 
appropriately registered or authorised by SAHPRA, such a health product may also be referred to the TAC, 
referred to in 5.2 above in cases where there is uncertainty and/or a dispute concerning the classification 
thereof. 
6. 
SUBMITTING AN APPLICATION 
An application form must be submitted to SAHPRA. The completed submission must include the following 
documentation: 
(a) Table of contents (paginated with hyperlinks to sections); 
(b) Cover letter; 
(c) Labelling including the professional information, patient information leaflet, and/or label (where 
applicable); 
(d) Information for use, user manual, marketing material, or brochure (where applicable); 
(e) Safety data sheet (where applicable); 
(f) Report of the results on developmental work (where applicable); 
(g) Proposed classification with supporting rationale, including composition of the product, primary and 
secondary (where applicable) mode of action with supporting evidence (where applicable), and any 
proposed claims (explicit and/or implicit); 
(h) Risk assessment file (in accordance with ISO 14971 in the case of medical devices and ICH Q9 Quality 
Risk Management Guidelines in the case of medicines) and, where applicable, a risk management 
plan;  
(i) Classification of the product in other jurisdictions recognised by SAHPRA (where relevant); 
(j) Proof of payment. 
The applicant must ensure that all relevant fields are completed, and all supporting documentation is attached. 
Incomplete applications will be identified as deficient, and review will not be progressed until deficiencies are 
addressed.

The prescribed fee and proof of payment must accompany the application. For the current fee payable, refer 
to the Regulations regarding fees payable in terms of the provisions of the Medicines and Related Substances 
Act, 1965 (Act 101 Of 1965) as published in the Government Gazette and on the SAHPRA website. 
The completed application should be submitted electronically to: borderlineproducts@sahpra.org.za using the

6.1 
Processing of applications  
Applications for classification of borderline products will be processed by SAHPRA within 30 working days 
of receiving a complete and valid submission.  
Upon receiving an application, SAHPRA will screen the application to ascertain whether all required 
documents as outlined above have been submitted. In cases where outstanding documents or other 
deficiencies are identified, SAHPRA will inform the applicant within 20 working days.  
Applicants will be allowed two opportunities to respond to queries. If the applicant fails to submit 
outstanding documents and/or address deficiencies within a stated timeframe, the application will be 
considered incomplete and rejected. Any applicable fee already paid will be forfeited.  
Once all required information has been received and evaluated, SAHPRA will issue a final response letter to 
the applicant confirming whether the product falls under the jurisdiction of SAHPRA or provide a 
recommendation as to the entity that could be approached for further advice. This letter will serve as 
SAHPRA’s official position on the classification of the product. 
Applicants who are aggrieved by the decision made by SAHPRA may appeal against such a decision in 
accordance with section 24A of the Medicines Act.2. 
If it is found that a product does fall under the jurisdiction of SAHPRA, a separate application for registration 
(for example, for a medicine) must be submitted as a separate process together with the applicable fee.
