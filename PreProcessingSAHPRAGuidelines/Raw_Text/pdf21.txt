1.
INTRODUCTION
Pre-clinical Testing
Information from pre-clinical testing is essential in planning clinical trials in humans.  For first-in-human (FIH)
trials, the information is used to decide what the starting dose should be, and the range of doses to be tested.
It also suggests what clinical signs and symptoms as well as laboratory abnormalities should be looked for to
detect any adverse effects, especially Serious Adverse Events (SAEs).
This document is intended to guide the type and extent of pre-clinical data to be provided in the Clinical Trial 
Application Form (CTF-1) for submission to SAHPRA for the conduct of a clinical trial in South Africa. Detailed 
data must be provided in the Investigator’s Brochure and cross-referenced from the CTF-1 where appropriate. 
In the CTF-1, the applicant is required to provide a summary of the pre-clinical testing that has been conducted 
during the development of a candidate medicinal or relevant related health products, and a discussion of how 
this supports the design of the clinical trial in the application. Pre-clinical testing may include both in vitro 
(laboratory), in silico (computer) and in vivo (animal) studies. 
The Guideline is adopted from ICH M3 (R2) non-clinical safety studies for the conduct of human clinical trials 
for pharmaceuticals - Scientific guideline2. 
SAHPRA supports the current international principles of the 3Rs - Reduction, Refinement, and Replacement – 
in animal testing, which involves animal pain, suffering, and/or death. However, in the development of new 
medicines certain animal tests remain essential for ensuring human safety before clinical investigation.  
Pre-clinical tests results supporting product functions and safety profile are particularly relevant to support FIH 
clinical trials but are also important support for subsequent and more extensive clinical trials where 
physiological or adverse effects observed in animal models can be important for the design of safety 
surveillance during later phases of clinical development.  
Pre-clinical test results can provide a preliminary assessment of the balance of risks versus potential benefits 
of the candidate investigational product (IP).

Animal testing can be used for elucidation of biodistribution, metabolism and elimination and evaluation of 
the investigational product activity if a suitable animal model is available.  Animal models should be validated 
and appropriate for the class of medicine/device being tested. 
Small-animal models, including mice and rats, may be useful for toxicity testing, but larger animal species are 
more appropriate for investigating physiological functions and may include rabbits, dogs, sheep, pigs and non-
human primates. 
Toxicity testing of pharmaceutical medicines should establish a No-Observed-Adverse-Effect-Level (NOAEL) in 
an appropriate animal model and may be determined during repeat dose toxicity studies. Pre-clinical testing 
of medical devices is necessary to establish safety and functional capabilities. Where animal models are 
needed, they must be qualified as appropriate for establishment of the required endpoint that maybe 
anatomical or physiological. 
In addition, for Biosimilar medicines, the investigational product must be fully characterised by comparative 
physico-chemical testing, demonstration of functional activity (e.g., receptor binding in cell cultures), and in 
vivo testing as “Biosimilar3” before it can be included in clinical trials.  SAHPRA may consider that for biosimilar 
medicines, established by comparative physico-chemical and functional similarity studies, in-vivo testing may 
be unnecessary. 
Clinical Factors in early Clinical Trials 
The Clinical factors section outlines the key clinical considerations relevant to dose selection, dose escalation, 
maximum exposure, and transition between single and multiple dosing regimens, with particular emphasis on 
healthy volunteer studies. It also addresses the planning and conduct of early clinical trials, including protocol 
design, participant selection, safety monitoring, data review, stopping rules, and communication of adverse 
events. In addition, the responsibilities of sponsors and investigators, as well as requirements for clinical site 
facilities and emergency preparedness, are described. Collectively, these principles aim to support informed 
decision-making, minimize risk, and ensure regulatory compliance throughout the early stages of clinical 
development.

7.1 Dose selection for FIH and early clinical trials
The starting dose and maximum exposure, as well as dose escalation steps during the clinical trial, should be 
justified and outlined in the protocol. The methods used and calculations on how doses and estimated

exposure levels were determined, including methods for modelling (e.g., PK/PD and physiologically based 
pharmacokinetic (PBPK)), should be summarised in the CTF-1 and protocol and the details included in the 
investigational brochure (IB).  
Deviations from the prespecified dose escalation and decision-making criteria would warrant the submission 
of substantial amendment(s). Decision making criteria for adapting dosage based on clinical data based on 
emerging clinical data should be described. 
In addition, sentinel dosing of two participants (one active, one placebo) is required at a minimum for the 
first single ascending dose (SAD) and multiple ascending dose (MAD) cohorts. 
7.1.1 Starting dose for Healthy Volunteers 
Sentinel safety cohorts are small groups of participants dosed before the main cohort.  These may help to 
identify potential adverse effects, and mitigate risk, especially in FIH and Phase 1 trials. 
The starting dose must mitigate the risk of toxicity while balancing the need to elicit pharmacologic activity.  
Reference should be made to published guidance documents from EMA and the FDA for investigators to 
follow when determining an appropriate starting dose. 
There are two methods that may be used for calculating the starting dose; an empirical or a mechanistic 
approach.  
The empirical approach aims at avoiding toxicity at initial human doses by using the NOAEL, a generally 
accepted benchmark for safety (FDA). The exposures achieved at the NOAEL in the most relevant animal 
species used (which might not necessarily be the most sensitive species) should be used for the estimation of 
an equivalent exposure for humans. 
The mechanistic approach is based on state-of-the-art modelling incorporating all relevant preclinical 
pharmacology data, including ex vivo and in vitro studies in human tissues (EMA).  
This approach enables the selection of a minimal anticipated biological effect dose-level, as this is becoming 
increasingly important as more targeted therapies are being developed that are very specific and potent, with 
predicted pharmacologically active doses well below a dose that is thought to be safe based on the NOAEL. 
7.1.2 Maximum Dose Selection 
The maximum exposure should be justified based on all available pre-clinical and clinical data, including PD, 
PK, findings in toxicity studies, and exposure at the expected therapeutic dose range. An expected maximum 
exposure level must not be exceeded in the study without approval of a substantial protocol amendment, 
this should have been pre-defined in the protocol for each study part.

7.1.3 Moving from single and multiple dosing 
The selection of an appropriate dosing interval and duration of dosing for all multiple dosing cohorts and 
study parts should consider the specific PK and PD characteristics of the IP, the available non-clinical safety 
data, and all data from participants in previous single-dose cohorts. 
A maximum duration of dosing should be stated in the protocol for every cohort. The expected exposure 
after multiple dosing (Cmax and AUC 0-tau) should have been covered during preceding SAD parts/trials. 
7.1.4 Route of Administration 
The choice of route of administration for dosing in humans should be based on the pre-clinical data, the 
characteristics of the IP, and the intended therapeutic use.  
7.2 Planning and conducting early clinical trials 
7.2.1 Protocol - Overall design 
The following are key considerations: 
a) Description of the trial and its ethical and scientific rationale. This would Include a description of what is
being done in the trial and the rationale behind key decisions so that the trial can be subject to scrutiny in
regulatory assessment.
b) Graphical representation of the overall scheme of the proposed trial in real-time, showing intervals for
interventions, sampling, timing of all reviews, and decision points.
c) Details on the size of the cohorts, including how many participants are on active IP and how many are on
placebo treatment.
d) Phase-1 clinical trials typically enroll small groups of participants; about 20 per cohort, and less than 100
for the whole trial.
7.2.2 Integrated protocol 
The practice of conducting FIH/early clinical trials with integrated protocols means that the information 
generated in previous parts needs to be analysed and integrated into an assessment in a limited timeframe 
before deciding on proceeding to the next part.  
All parts, and the criteria to move from one part to another, should be predefined within an integrated 
protocol, as should possible modifications, based on the totality of available information and the related 
uncertainty. 
Recommendations for time sequence for the conduct of different parts: 
a)
Overlapping of SAD and MAD parts may be acceptable. The proposed overlap, however, should be
scientifically justified and supported by decision points and a review of available data before starting
the MAD part.

b) Other single-dose parts (e.g., food interaction) could be conducted in parallel to the SAD part,
provided the dose chosen and the expected exposure are equal to or lower than that which was

7.2.3 Choice of participants 
The choice to conduct FIH trials in healthy participants or patients should be made on a case‐by‐case basis. The 
risks of enrolling healthy volunteers versus patients must be weighed, including toxicities, PK variability, lifestyle conditions, 
benefits to patients, and special populations. 
Clinical factors to consider in the decision to conduct a study in healthy volunteers or patients include:  
a)
Whether the toxicities foreseen/risks associated can support the inclusion of healthy volunteers.
b)
The relative presence of the molecular target in healthy participants or in patients.
c)
The possible variable PK, PD or safety profile in participants.
d)
The potential differences between the targeted patient group and healthy volunteers.
e)
Possible interactions with participants’ lifestyle, e.g., smoking, use of alcohol or recreational drugs.
f)
The use of other medications with the possibility of adverse reactions and/or difficulties in the
interpretation of results.
g)
A potential of patients to benefit from other products or interventions.
h)
The predicted therapeutic window of the IP based on preclinical testing.
i)
Factors relating to special populations, including age, gender, ethnicity, and genotype(s).
Key inclusion and exclusion criteria for trials involving healthy participants should include an adequate set of 
vital signs (including ECG), laboratory values and clinical assessments that should be within normal ranges. A 
justification for deviations outside the defined normal ranges should be provided.  
7.2.4 Safety monitoring and interventions 
The detection of a safety signal with a high degree of certainty is extremely limited by the typically small size 
of these trials. Successful signal detection in FIH studies depends on adequate collection of data coupled to a 
systematic analysis of the data, a determination of the strength of a potential signal, and an understanding of 
the quantitative limitations of typical study designs. 
The participant’s safety assessments that will be routinely conducted, their timing, and any additional 
monitoring actions or interventions should be pre-specified in line with the known pharmacological and non-

clinical safety profile and balanced against the degree of uncertainty.  Any proposal to routinely omit an 
assessment should be scientifically based.  
The length of follow-up of participants should be specified within the protocol (e.g., for possible delayed 
adverse reactions). 
The sponsor should describe how safety monitoring should be extended until parameters return to within 
the normal range or to baseline, as appropriate for the population. 
Extended monitoring should be considered, e.g., when the mechanism entails enzyme inhibition or activation 
(monitoring should continue until enzyme activity has returned to baseline or to an acceptable percentage of 
baseline) or when prolonged PD effects are observed, regardless of duration of target inhibition or the PK 
profile of the IP. 
Repeated assessments, integrating available knowledge with rapid processing of emerging information, are 
crucial for the recognition of developing toxicity at an early stage.

•
Evaluation of data by a defined expert group and summary of criteria for evaluation and progression
to the next dose-level.
•
‘Evaluable’ participants should be defined, i.e., participants who have completed all planned study
visits at least until the time of the decision as detailed in the protocol.
•
Data collection as planned in the protocol in each dosing cohort should be complete and the decision
to proceed to the next dose cohort should be documented.

7.2.7 Monitoring and communication of adverse events
For all clinical trials, adverse events of special interest (AESI) should be defined, based on non-clinical findings

Due to high risk of Phase 1 /FIH trials, four weekly progress reports on a SAHPRA template should be
submitted.
7.3  Documentation of sponsor and investigational responsibilities 
The responsibilities of the sponsor and investigator(s) (as well as any other experts or study staff) in decision-
making should be clearly defined in the protocol. Responsibility regarding breaking the treatment code in 
emergencies should also be documented. 
The composition of any decision-making group or safety review committee should be documented in the 
protocol. Other details to include are the exact remit of the group and the roles of all members in the 
committee, and their relation to the sponsor. Consideration should be given to the inclusion of independent 
experts who are (at least) external to the study. Written statements and conclusions by any decision-making 
or safety review group must be in place before allowing trial progression at the noted times as per protocol. 
This includes documentation of appropriate quality control checks on the data reviewed. 
7.3.1 Investigator site facilities/personnel 
To prioritize participant safety and data integrity, FIH clinical trials should be undertaken in appropriate clinical

the special characterisƟcs of the IP and its mechanism of acƟon are crucial.
